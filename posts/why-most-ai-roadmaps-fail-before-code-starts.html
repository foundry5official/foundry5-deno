Why most AI roadmaps fail before code starts

Understanding why advisory decks stall out and what engineering reality looks like instead.

27 October 2026

The trap of the theoretical roadmap

When a technology catches fire across the market, organisations rush to produce a plan. Teams spend weeks in workshops with advisors who understand enterprise frameworks far better than they understand vector databases or token context limits. The resulting document is thick with ambition, beautiful transition slides, and high-level architecture diagrams that suggest artificial intelligence will solve operational friction overnight.

Yet this approach hides a fundamental flaw. These plans are rarely written by the people who have to build, deploy, and maintain the software under production load. They skip over the messy reality of data cleanliness, latency constraints, and the true cost of inference at scale. A leadership team reads the roadmap, nods along with the conclusions because they sound sophisticated, and then hands the document to internal developers who quietly realise half the assumptions defy physics or current API limits. Nothing gets built because the strategy was designed to satisfy a board meeting rather than a codebase.

The bias in build-versus-buy decisions

Another major hurdle appears when deciding how to source the technology. Asking a third-party platform whether you should build your own system or subscribe to their service is like asking a car salesperson if you really need a new vehicle. The recommendation almost always leans toward buying what they sell, regardless of whether your product data requires strict on-premise residency or custom fine-tuning that off-the-shelf tools cannot provide.

This decision carries heavy financial consequences that extend far beyond initial licensing fees. A platform that looks inexpensive at low user volumes can become prohibitively expensive once data throughput multiplies. Conversely, building an in-house model pipeline without adequate engineering oversight drains engineering hours on foundational problems that solved nothing unique to the business. Navigating this balance requires an independent assessment of accuracy, latency, and data privacy constraints without vendor influence tipping the scales.

Moving from slide decks to shipped software

Surviving the initial planning phase requires shifting the focus away from generic transformation jargon and toward concrete engineering truths. Boards and investors are increasingly asking probing questions about data readiness, regulatory compliance under UK GDPR, and how inference costs scale alongside revenue growth. They want answers that survive a rigorous internal audit rather than optimistic projections meant for a fundraising round.
